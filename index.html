What Counts as a Dental Emergency?

Not every dental problem is an emergency, but some situations should never be ignored. In general, you should seek urgent dental treatment if you have any of the following:

  • Severe or throbbing tooth pain that does not settle
  • Swelling in the gums, face or jaw
  • A knocked-out or loose permanent tooth
  • A badly cracked or broken tooth
  • Bleeding from the mouth that will not stop
  • A pimple-like bump on the gums, a bad taste or other signs of infection
  • A lost crown or filling with pain or sharp edges

A small chip without pain may be able to wait a short time, but it is always safer to call and ask. If you are unsure, call 856-335-2038. We would rather answer a question and tell you that you can wait until morning than have you suffer at home or let a small problem grow. Booking an urgent dental appointment early can often prevent a bigger procedure later.

Toothache Relief: Finding the Cause and Stopping the Pain

A toothache is the most common reason people look for an emergency dentist. The pain can be sharp, dull, constant or only when you chew or drink something hot or cold. Whatever the pattern, pain is your body's warning that something is wrong. The cause may be deep decay, a cracked tooth, a failing filling, gum disease, an impacted wisdom tooth or an infection inside the tooth.

Effective toothache relief starts with finding the real cause. Painkillers can take the edge off for a few hours, but they do not fix the source. During an emergency visit, your dentist will examine the tooth, take x-rays when needed and check the surrounding gums and bone. Once the cause is known, treatment can range from a simple filling to a root canal or extraction.

Until you can be seen, rinse gently with warm salt water, keep your head elevated when resting and avoid very hot, cold or sugary foods. Over-the-counter pain medicine can be used as directed on the label, and aspirin should never be placed directly on the gums because it can burn the tissue. These steps may ease discomfort, but they are temporary, so call for professional dental pain relief as soon as you can.

Dental Abscess Treatment: Do Not Wait on an Infection

A dental abscess is a pocket of infection near the root of a tooth or in the gums. Symptoms include throbbing pain, swelling in the face or jaw, tenderness when biting, a bad taste and sometimes fever. Some abscesses seem to go away when the pocket drains, but the infection is usually still there and can return stronger.

Prompt dental abscess treatment matters because infection can spread to nearby tissues. If you have facial swelling together with fever, trouble swallowing or trouble breathing, treat it as a serious medical emergency and go to a hospital emergency room right away. For a localized abscess, treatment may include draining the infection, a root canal, removal of a tooth that cannot be saved and, when appropriate, medication. The sooner you are seen, the simpler the treatment usually is.

Emergency Tooth Extraction: When Removing a Tooth Is the Best Choice

Dentists always try to save natural teeth, but sometimes removal is the safest option. A tooth may be too badly broken, too decayed or too damaged by infection or gum disease to repair. In those cases, an emergency tooth extraction can stop the pain, remove the source of infection and protect the teeth around it.

During the procedure, the area is numbed so you stay comfortable. Afterward, you receive clear healing instructions: avoid straws and smoking, eat soft foods, keep the area clean and watch for signs of problems. We can also discuss ways to replace the missing tooth later, such as a bridge or implant, so your bite and smile stay healthy.

Emergency Root Canal: Saving a Tooth Instead of Losing It

The words "root canal" worry many people, but the treatment is designed to relieve pain, not cause it. When the soft tissue inside a tooth becomes infected or inflamed, the pain can be intense. An emergency root canal removes the infected tissue, cleans the inside of the tooth and seals it, which lets you keep your natural tooth in most cases.

Saving the tooth has real benefits. It keeps your natural chewing surface, protects the neighboring teeth and avoids the need for a replacement. A crown is often recommended afterward to strengthen the treated tooth. Modern anesthesia makes the procedure far more comfortable than its reputation suggests.

Broken Tooth Repair and Chipped Tooth Fix

Teeth can break from biting hard foods, falls, sports injuries, car accidents or old fillings that weaken over time. A small chip may feel rough on your tongue, while a larger fracture can expose the sensitive inner layers and cause sharp pain. Quick broken tooth repair protects the tooth from further damage and infection.

Depending on the size and location of the break, a chipped tooth fix may involve smoothing the edge, bonding with tooth-colored material, a filling, a veneer or a crown, or treating the nerve if it has been exposed. If you break a tooth, rinse your mouth with warm water, save any pieces, apply a cold compress to reduce swelling and call us right away. Avoid chewing on that side until you are seen.

Dental Trauma Repair: Knocked-Out and Injured Teeth

A blow to the mouth can loosen, push, crack or completely knock out a tooth. Dental trauma repair is time sensitive, especially when a permanent tooth is knocked out. Acting within the first hour gives the best chance of saving it.

If a permanent tooth is knocked out, pick it up by the crown (the chewing part), not the root. If it is dirty, rinse it briefly with water without scrubbing. If possible, gently place it back in the socket and hold it there. If that is not possible, keep it moist in milk and get care immediately. Do not wrap it in a dry tissue. Even if the tooth cannot be saved, a quick visit lets us control bleeding, check for other injuries and plan the next steps.

Managing the Cost of Emergency Dental Care

Cost is a real concern for many families, and it should not stop you from getting help. Delaying care often leads to bigger and more expensive treatment, because a small cavity can become an infection and a cracked tooth can become a lost tooth. Seeing a dentist early is often the most economical choice.

To keep costs reasonable, ask for an explanation of every option, and ask what must be done today and what can be planned for later. If you have dental insurance, bring your card and ask what it covers. If you do not, ask about payment options. What to Do Before You Reach the Dentist

A few simple steps can protect your tooth and make your visit smoother:

  • Call first. Phone 856-335-2038 so we can guide you.
  • Rinse gently. Use warm water or a mild salt-water rinse.
  • Reduce swelling. Apply a cold compress to the outside of the cheek for short periods.
  • Save pieces. Keep broken fragments or a knocked-out tooth moist and bring them with you.
  • Be careful with food. Avoid chewing on the painful side and avoid very hot, cold or sugary items.
  • Share your history. Tell us about your medications, allergies and health conditions.

When to Go to the Hospital Instead

Most dental emergencies can be handled by a dentist, but some situations need immediate medical care. Go to an emergency room or call 911 if you have trouble breathing or swallowing, swelling that spreads toward your eye or neck, a high fever together with facial swelling or a serious injury to the head or jaw. After you are stable, we can help with the dental side of your care.

How to Prevent the Next Dental Emergency

Many emergencies can be prevented with simple habits. Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, floss daily and visit your dentist regularly so small problems are found early. Wear a mouthguard during sports, avoid chewing ice or opening packages with your teeth and replace old fillings before they fail. If you grind your teeth at night, ask about a night guard. Pay attention to early warning signs such as sensitivity or bleeding gums. They often appear weeks before a full emergency, and treating them early is easier, more comfortable and less costly.
